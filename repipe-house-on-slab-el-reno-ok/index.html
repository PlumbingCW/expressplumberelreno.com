How to Repipe a House on a Slab in El Reno, OK

Repiping a house on a slab sounds like jackhammer work, but most of the time it is not. The standard approach is to leave the old lines in the concrete, cap them, and run new supply lines through the attic and walls. That avoids breaking the foundation.

This guide explains the method, the materials and the questions to ask before you hire anyone.

The method

Reroute through the attic

On a slab home, the supply lines that feed fixtures often run under the concrete, which is why a leak there is hard to reach. A repipe abandons those lines and runs new ones overhead. The plumber opens small access holes in walls and ceilings, pulls new pipe to each fixture and reconnects.

The drain lines stay in the slab, since they are larger and do not usually leak under pressure. If a drain is also failing, that is a separate job. See sewer line repair.

Reasons

When repiping makes sense

Repeated leaks in different spots, brown water, weak pressure and old polybutylene or galvanized pipe are common reasons. A single leak in a sound system may not justify a repipe. Compare the cost of repeated spot repairs against a full replacement, and ask the plumber to inspect the pipe and show you the condition.

See our repiping page for the decision guide, and consider leak detection first if you are unsure where the problem lies.

Process

What the job looks like

The plumber walks the house, counts fixtures and plans routes. Water is shut off during parts of the work, and you may need to move furniture away from walls. New lines are run and tested at pressure before the openings are closed. Patching and painting may be separate.

Ask who handles the patching, how long water will be off and when the inspection happens. Time varies with house size and access.

Costs

What affects the price

The number of fixtures, the pipe material, how easy the attic is to reach, the layout of the house and the amount of wall repair all shape the price. A single-story ranch with a roomy attic is easier than a house with a low-pitch roof and finished ceilings.

Get a written quote that lists materials, openings, patching and permit status. A very low quote may omit wall repair or the permit.

Living through it

What to expect at home

Expect dust, noise and short water shutoffs. Cover furniture, remove items from walls and plan for pets. Keep a few gallons of water for drinking and flushing on the day of the work. After the repipe, flush the lines at each fixture to clear debris.

Keep the plumber's warranty and any permit documents with your house records. They matter at resale.

Questions to ask

Ten minutes of questions before you sign

Ask which pipe material the plumber proposes and why. Ask whether the old lines will be capped or left open, and how the capped lines are protected. Ask whether the quote includes patching drywall and painting, or whether another contractor does that.

Ask for a written schedule, the permit status and the warranty on the work and on the pipe. Ask what happens to your water service each day, and whether a pressure test is done before the walls are closed. A plumber who has done this work will answer without hesitation.

Alternatives

Options other than a full repipe

Sometimes the right answer is smaller. If one slab leak is the only problem and the pipe is otherwise sound, a spot repair or a single-line reroute can cost less and solve it. Our slab leak repair page compares these options. A plumber can also pressure test each line to show which are failing.

Epoxy lining is another option on some systems, where a coating is applied inside the existing pipe. It does not suit every pipe or every house. A plumber should explain the condition of the pipe before recommending lining, and should be clear about the warranty.

Local note

Repiping rules in El Reno

El Reno adopts the International Plumbing Code (IPC) with local amendments, expects permits for work like this. Ask El Reno Community Development at 405-262-2000 about permit and inspection rules, and confirm your plumber's license on the Oklahoma Construction Industries Board lookup.

Slab homes are common in Yukon and Piedmont too. Many Yukon homes date from the 1970s through the 1990s, when copper and, in some homes, polybutylene pipe were used, so the age of your house is a clue to what is under it.
